# Research playbook — finding people

Read this when a person's trail goes cold. The aim is never volume; it's the two or three specific facts that make a card worth reading.

## Career, role and reporting line

| Source | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Company leadership/governance page | Authoritative titles and, sometimes, the structure |
| **Appointment press releases** | The most underused source. They state the reporting line explicitly ("will report to the CEO and join the Executive Committee") — which the website almost never does. Search `"<name>" appointed OR joins OR named`. |
| Annual report / URD | Exec committee composition, tenure, remit, sometimes compensation (a hard signal of relative power) |
| LinkedIn | Tenure, prior roles, how *they* describe the job — often more revealing than the official title |
| The Org, Crunchbase people pages | Structure and org size. Frequently stale — cross-check before trusting, and tag `[C]`. |
| Conference speaker bios | Written by the person; tells you what they want to be known for |

**Reporting line is the highest-value fact on the card.** A CDO under the COO is an implementer; a CDO under the CEO is a mandate. Chase it.

## What they've actually done

- Search the person's name with the products/deals they claim: `"<name>" launched OR migration OR acquisition`.
- Press releases where they are *quoted* — companies quote the person accountable for the thing.
- Earnings-call and CMD transcripts: search for their name and their business line. What management defends under questioning is what they own.
- Prior employer's press coverage from their tenure. A "transformation leader" whose previous employer's programme quietly died is a different proposition.
- Look for the failures too. They're public more often than you'd think, and they're the best predictor of what someone will be cautious about.

## Public convictions

This is the raw material for the opening message, so gather it last, when you know what matters.

- Keynotes and panels (often on YouTube; the description and press write-ups are usually enough)
- LinkedIn posts — executives telegraph strategy far more than they realise
- Op-eds, trade-press interviews (payments: Finextra, The Paypers; banking: Sifted, Les Échos)
- Podcasts — people say things there they'd never put in a press release
- Quote it, date it, source it. An undated quote is unusable in a room.

## Allies and counterweights — from the record only

Legitimate evidence, all publicly verifiable:

- **Hires that followed them** from a previous employer. Loyalty travels, and it is documented on LinkedIn.
- **Long overlaps** — years spent at the same firm in adjacent roles.
- **Co-sponsorship** of an initiative, joint appearances, joint quotes in the same release.
- **Reorganisations** — a remit expanded at another's expense is a structural fact. Announcements state who gained what.
- **Documented disagreements**: divergent public positions, a resignation following a decision, an exec-committee departure timed to a strategy shift.

Frame the finding as structure, never as psychology. "Their remits overlap on the platform roadmap, and the 2025 reorg moved data science from his org to hers" is checkable and useful. "There's bad blood" is neither, and it's the kind of line that makes the person carrying the document look foolish when it turns out to be wrong.

If the record is silent, write **"no public signal"**. That's an honest and genuinely informative answer.

## Email pattern — where real examples hide

You need *observed* addresses at the domain before you can infer anything:

- Press releases — media/press contacts, often a named person
- Conference programmes and speaker listings
- Academic papers, whitepapers, standards-body submissions (payments, telco and health are full of these)
- Regulatory filings and consultation responses
- PDF document metadata and properties
- GitHub commit history (if the company has any public repos)
- Job postings and support pages
- Registrar/WHOIS technical contacts (rarely useful, occasionally decisive)

Then:

1. Establish the **domain** — the corporate domain often differs from the consumer brand, and groups frequently keep separate domains per legal entity. Get this wrong and the confidence score is meaningless.
2. Derive the **pattern**: `firstname.lastname@`, `f.lastname@`, `firstnamelastname@`, `firstname@`.
3. Watch for **name traps**: accents (é → e), particles (van, de, von — sometimes kept, sometimes dropped), double-barrelled surnames, married names, and anglicised first names. These are where confident guesses die; when in doubt, lower the score and offer the variant.
4. Score by *pattern strength*, not by how much you'd like the address to be right.

Two hard rules: **professional addresses only** — if you stumble on a personal account, do not use it; and **office/city location only** — never a home address. Beyond the legal question, a brief containing someone's private details is one your reader cannot safely forward to a colleague, which destroys its purpose.

## The opening message

Reread their convictions, pick the most recent specific thing, and write to that. The test: **could this message be sent to anyone else in their sector?** If yes, throw it away and start again.
