# The 8 modules, explained — with the Northwind reading

This document sets out what each module tests, why it matters, which acronym means what,
and what the module returned on Northwind (rev. 2). Read it alongside
`scoring-rubric.md`, which gives the 0–5 anchors.

**Northwind** is a disguised composite of a real diligence, used throughout as a worked reading.

**General principle.** Each module is a *question to settle*, not a chapter to fill.
If you can write the section without a single sentence the founder could contradict,
you have described the company instead of diligencing it.

**The three confidence tags**, carried by every figure in the memo:
`[A]` filed accounts, invoices, or data we recomputed ourselves ·
`[B]` first-hand interview, company statement, credible trade press ·
`[C]` estimated, inferred, or single-source. Never mix the three in the same sentence.

---

## 1. Problem & value proposition

**The question.** What "job" is this product hired for? The framing comes from *Jobs to be
Done*: a customer does not buy a product, they hire it to move a situation forward.

**The test.** Ask 5 or more customers to describe the value, without leading them. If the
vocabulary converges, there is a positioning. If it diverges, there is only a set of features and
each customer has told themselves their own story.

**What we are really looking for.** Painkiller or vitamin — a painkiller is bought on a constrained
budget, a vitamin on a discretionary one, and discretionary goes first.
And above all: does the **buyer** feel the pain, or only the **user**? When the user suffers and the
buyer sees nothing, sales cycles stretch indefinitely.

**Northwind: 4/5 `[B]`.** This is the strong point and it is independently confirmed. Our
France-side interviewee (I-01) describes the value exactly as the website frames it: the platform
exposes every available transport route with, for each one, the product category accepted,
the lead time, the cost and the carbon footprint per kilometre — and arbitrates between them. That is
a precise and painful job: the last mile is 30 to 50% of transport cost. The buyer (supply chain
director) carries that cost in their objectives. This is not a vitamin.

## 2. Segmentation & ICP

**ICP = Ideal Customer Profile**: the type of customer the product works best and longest for.

**The question.** Not "who do you sell to" (demographics: size, sector, country) but **"who behaves
the same way and pays for the same reasons"**. Good segmentation is behavioural and grounded in
willingness to pay.

**The test.** Two figures side by side: revenue concentration by segment, and retention by
segment. Then one question: is the segment that retains best the one the sales team targets? The gap
between the two is **the single most frequent discovery in all of product due
diligence**. An aggregate figure hiding one good segment and two bad ones looks like a mediocre
company when there is a good company inside it — or the reverse.

**Northwind: 2/5 `[B]`.** Northwind sells simultaneously to omnichannel retail, B2B industrials,
carriers and partner SaaS vendors. Average revenue per customer (~€13k: €5.14M of
FY24 revenue across the ~400 customers claimed) gives away a mixed base: a few large accounts plus a
long tail that has no business being there. The interviews pin down the real dividing line, and it is
not sectoral: **the customer who brings their own carrier does not consume the same product** as
the one who comes for the network. The reference account, Belgium, was in the first case — same carrier,
same account ID — and so had structurally no reason to stay (I-02).

## 3. PMF evidence

**PMF = Product-Market Fit.** This is the most heavily weighted module from Series A onward,
because everything else depends on it.

**The four proofs, in order of strength:**

- **Does the cohort retention curve flatten?** Group customers by month of
  acquisition (the cohort), and track what percentage is still active at M+1, M+2, M+3… If the curve
  falls and then **settles onto a plateau**, there is a core of customers who will not leave:
  that is PMF. If it falls continuously, there is no bottom to the bucket, only a sales
  team filling it faster than it leaks. **A flat curve beats any growth
  number.**
- **Usage frequency.** `DAU/MAU` = Daily Active Users / Monthly Active Users, daily
  actives over monthly actives — a stickiness ratio. The ratio in itself does not matter: what
  matters is comparing it to **the natural frequency of the job**. A payroll tool used once a
  month is perfectly sticky; a delivery tool used once a month is not.
- **Organic vs paid.** What share of acquisition arrives with no marketing spend? And its
  trend. Acquisition that is 100% paid is not evidence of traction, it is a budget.
- **The Sean Ellis score.** One question asked of users: "how would you feel
  if this product disappeared tomorrow — very disappointed / somewhat disappointed / not
  disappointed?". Above ~40% "very disappointed" **within the ICP**, PMF is considered
  present. It is a signal, not a verdict.

**Northwind: 1/5 `[B]`.** The lowest score on the board, and it fell in rev. 2. There is still
no cohort file — but we now have something better (or worse): direct evidence.
The reference account most cited publicly, sitting inside the shareholder ecosystem itself,
left in Belgium for an internal tool (I-02), and its sponsor states that it never scaled
(I-03). The status of the France account is unconfirmed, including by our own
France-side interviewee (I-01). We have moved from "absence of evidence" to "adverse evidence".

## 4. Market & sizing

Three nested acronyms: **TAM** = Total Addressable Market, the theoretical total market;
**SAM** = Serviceable Addressable Market, the share the product can technically serve;
**SOM** = Serviceable Obtainable Market, the share actually reachable given the
competition and the resources.

**The rule.** *Bottom-up* only: number of real accounts × realistic ACV × credible penetration
rate, every factor sourced. **ACV = Annual Contract Value**, the annual value of one customer
contract. Throw out *top-down* slides of the "the global market is $7bn, we take 1%" kind.
That reasoning has never sold a contract.

**The test that counts.** Not "how big is the SOM" but **"what would have to be true to
reach that SOM in 3 years, and is there anything in the current business that proves it?"**

**The pyramid.** For this memo I added a pyramid representation: customer segments stacked from
the apex (few actors, most of the value) down to the base (tens of thousands
of actors, little value each), with the actor count and the share of spend at each tier.
It serves one purpose: to show that **the tier determines the competitors and the price**. A product
built for the apex and priced like the base does not have a sales problem, it has a model
problem.

**Northwind: 3/5 `[B/C]`.** The domestic apex is real and concentrated: some thirty major
omnichannel distributors account for roughly 45% of delivery spend. That is a reachable SOM
in theory. But current international expansion runs through an entity of the same shareholder
— and export is only 8.7% of FY24 revenue `[A]` despite the
communication about "20 countries". The figure of 158,200 active merchant sites comes from FEVAD
(the national e-commerce and distance-selling federation) `[B]`; the distribution of value by tier
remains our estimate `[C]`, and sits at priority 3 in the appendix.

## 5. Differentiation & moat

*Moat*, literally the ditch around a castle: what stops a competitor from getting in.

**Two stages.** First the feature-parity grid against 3–5 named competitors: where we are
ahead, where behind, today. Then the real question, far harder: **what will still be
defensible in 24 months?**

**What is almost never a moat**: a feature. It gets copied.
**What is one:**
- **switching costs** — the customer would stay even if they preferred something else,
  because leaving costs too much;
- **data network effects** — the product improves mechanically as it is
  used, so the latecomer never catches up;
- **distribution** — a position of access to the customer that a competitor cannot buy.

**And one question everyone forgets to ask: can the customer build it themselves?**
At the apex of the pyramid, the competitor is often not a software vendor, it is the customer's own IT
department.

**Northwind: 2/5 `[B]`, down in rev. 2.** On paper the moat is the network: 600 to 1,000
pre-connected carriers, plus an acquired routing-optimisation engine held as proprietary
technology. The interviews cut it down sharply. The reference account,
Belgium, used its own carrier and its own account ID: **the network was never activated**. Where it
is not, Northwind supplies only the interface layer — label generation — and that layer was
taken back in-house by a modernised in-house tool. The structuring competitor at the apex
is therefore neither of the two category incumbents: it is the internal build. And it removes from the
addressable market precisely those distributors that negotiate their own transport contracts — that
is, the target.

## 6. Monetization

**The central concept: alignment of the *pricing metric* with the *value metric*.** The *value metric*
is the unit in which the customer receives value; the *pricing metric*, the unit in which
they pay. If the two are aligned, the customer who triples their value triples their bill, and revenue
growth happens on its own. If they are misaligned — per-seat pricing when the value is in
volume processed, for example — **NRR is structurally capped** and no amount of sales effort
will unblock it.

**NRR = Net Revenue Retention**: the revenue generated today by the
customers present 12 months ago, over their revenue back then. Above 100%, the existing base
grows on its own. **GRR = Gross Revenue Retention**: the same thing *without* counting
expansion. Always look at both: a flattering NRR can mask a catastrophic GRR, where
two large accounts growing hide a haemorrhage of small ones.

We also look at *packaging* (how features are grouped into offers) and
discount discipline.

**Northwind: 2/5, but now in `[A]`.** An invoice we obtained and recomputed settles it: Northwind bills
**€0.75 per transport order created on the platform** — 4,779 orders for Belgium in April
2025, i.e. €3,584.25 excl. tax. The metric is *theoretically* right: it tracks volume delivered, hence
value. Two problems in practice, both raised unprompted by two different
interviewees. First, the unit price appears identical whether the customer consumes the full
orchestration and the network, or only label printing — hence the "too expensive" from I-02, at
€3,500–5,000 per month for a single country. Second, being indexed to volume **penalises exactly
the ramp-up phase**, when a new shipping model is fragile: "in a difficult
ramp-up, hard to make it pay" (I-03). The value-creation lever is therefore
not the price level, it is the packaging: separate the connectivity tier from the
orchestration + network tier. But that reduces revenue before it increases it.

## 7. GTM–product fit

**GTM = Go-To-Market**, the way you reach the market: who sells, how, at what cost.
**PLG = Product-Led Growth** — the customer signs up, tries and
buys alone, with no salesperson.

**The question.** Does the sales motion match the ACV? The two symmetrical mistakes
destroy margin just as surely as each other: PLG on an €80k contract (nobody
signs a commitment of that size alone), or a field sales force on a €500 product (the
cost of acquisition exceeds the value of the customer).

**The test.** **CAC payback** by channel and by segment. *CAC = Customer Acquisition Cost*, the cost
of acquiring one customer; *payback* is the number of months of gross margin needed to
repay it. Below 18 months, the machine can feed itself; beyond 30, every new
customer worsens the cash need. A second test, less quantitative but highly predictive:
**does the product sell without the founder in the room?**

**Northwind: 2/5 `[A/B]`.** Salaries are 172% of FY24 revenue `[A]` — the
structure is carried by the shareholder, not by the customers. Export is capped at 8.7% `[A]`.
And interview I-03 explains what the accounts do not say: "very hard to get out of
France", with **very long carrier onboarding when that was the founding asset**.
In other words, the differentiating asset is also the bottleneck in getting live. The
CEO recruited in 2025 precisely on the B2B and international mandate left after seven months.

## 8. Product org & velocity

**The question.** This module does not judge today's product but **the capacity to execute
tomorrow's plan**. It is the one that says whether a value-creation plan is realistic or decorative.

**What we look at.** The shape of the team (product / design / research ratio per engineer); the
*discovery* practices — does the team talk to users, or build the loudest
request?; the coherence between the roadmap and the stated strategy — if the roadmap is in
fact the largest customer's backlog, it is not a strategy; and **technical debt
expressed as a product constraint**: not "the code is old" but "we cannot
ship X until Y is rebuilt".

**Northwind: 3/5 `[B]`.** Positive and real signals: a route-simulation module designed at an internal
hackathon in late 2025 and launched at a sector trade show in March 2026, a cost-management module added over the same period, public
API documentation maintained across three surfaces, release notes. A team that ships.
Negative signals: headcount down from about 130 to 80 in a year, and **closure of the historic R&D site
in June 2026** `[A]` — that is, the historic R&D base of the route-optimisation engine, hence of
the technology asset hardest to copy. Hence the appendix line: engineering headcount
by module, before and after restructuring.

## How to read the total

The weights are not fixed: they depend on the stage and the fund's thesis (table in
`scoring-rubric.md`). On Northwind I applied a *growth* profile: PMF, moat, monetization and GTM at 3,
segmentation and market at 2, value proposition and org at 1–2. The weighted total comes out
at **2.2 / 5**, below any growth investment bar.

What matters more than the total is **where the weak points sit**. Here they are clustered on
PMF, moat and monetization — three structural modules, not execution ones. An execution problem is
fixed with a good operator; a problem of defensible proposition is only fixed by changing
product or segment. That is what moves the verdict from *Conditional* to *Pass*, with an
explicit reopening condition: a cohort file showing that a Tier-1 account **outside the
ecosystem** consumes the network and expands.
