# How each channel actually behaves

Read this when planning the sequence. The point isn't to use every channel — it's to pick the two or three that fit *this* person and be honest about the rest.

## Warm introduction — always look here first

The highest-converting route by a wide margin, and the one most plans skip because it takes work to find.

Where a path may exist: shared former employers (LinkedIn shows this), investors and board members in common, trade bodies and working groups, conference co-panellists, the company's own partners and suppliers, alumni networks, open-source or standards contributions.

Two rules. **Name the connector** — "someone in your network might know him" is not a plan. And **write the forwardable paragraph for them**: three sentences the connector can paste without editing. The friction in a warm intro is never willingness, it's the effort of composing it. Remove that and the intro happens.

If no path exists, say so and move on. A fabricated connection is the single fastest way to be permanently blacklisted.

## The public event — the highest-value research you'll do

A face and a voice outrank an email forever. After thirty seconds in person, you are no longer a stranger, and every subsequent email is answered.

**Finding them**: `"<name>" keynote OR panel OR speaker 2026`; the sector's main conference agendas; their LinkedIn (executives announce their appearances, often weeks ahead); the employer's events page; trade-body working groups; awards juries; university guest lectures.

**Read the role, it changes everything:**

- **Keynote** — they'll be mobbed immediately after, then whisked away. Hard. Catch them later at the drinks, or don't bother.
- **Panellist** — the sweet spot. Panellists come off stage under-consulted and slightly under-used. They have time and they're still warm from talking.
- **Chair / moderator** — approachable, and they know everyone in the room. Even if they're not the target, a chair can introduce you to one.
- **Attending only** — hardest to locate in a crowd, but the most relaxed if you do.

**The approach itself**: lead with a question about what they just said. Not a compliment, not a business card, not what you do. Let them ask what you do — and they will, because reciprocity is reflexive. If they don't, you weren't interesting enough, and the pitch wouldn't have saved you.

**Access**: check whether it's open, ticketed, invite-only or industry-restricted, and roughly what it costs. Always look for the cheaper way in — a free side event, a sponsor pass, a public session, the bar of the conference hotel.

## Direct email

Low base rate, high variance. It works exactly to the extent that the message could not have been sent to anyone else.

- Send **Tuesday–Thursday, early morning** in their timezone. Senior people clear their own inbox before the day starts; by mid-morning an assistant may be triaging.
- **Subject line is half the email.** A concrete thing, not a category. Lowercase often reads as human rather than as a campaign.
- **Under 120 words.** Every extra line lowers the reply rate; this is the most reliable finding in the whole discipline.
- **One ask.** Two asks is zero asks.
- No attachments, no decks, no links on first contact — they trip filters and they signal a campaign.

## LinkedIn

Better than email for people who don't run their own inbox, worse for those who do.

- A connection request **with a note** beats an InMail. Keep the note to two sentences.
- If they post, **comment first, message second.** Commenting is public, low-cost to them, and makes your name familiar before the ask arrives.
- Never open with a pitch in the request. It's the fastest possible route to being ignored permanently.

## Public commentary — the slow disarming route

For people who publish. You comment thoughtfully on two or three posts over a fortnight, adding something rather than agreeing. By the time you message, you're not a stranger — you're the person with the good takes.

Slow, but it converts precisely because it costs you something and asks nothing. Add something the author didn't say. "Great post!" is worse than silence: it costs nothing and signals nothing.

## Inbound content — the slowest and strongest

The user publishes something the target would want to read — an analysis of the exact problem the target owns. They come to you. This inverts the entire power dynamic of the conversation and it is the only route that scales past one person.

Only viable if the user can actually write or has something worth saying. Don't recommend it as filler.

## The gatekeeper — consistently underrated

Chiefs of staff and executive assistants are not obstacles; they are the routing layer, and they are professionals at judging what deserves their principal's time.

A short, respectful, unmistakably specific note to an EA — stating who you are, why this is relevant to their principal *now*, and exactly how much time you're asking for — beats a cold email to the principal. They will forward what's genuinely relevant, because that's the job. What they filter out is what's vague.

## Timing across the sequence

- **D+0** signal (public, no ask) → **D+5–10** approach (the real message) → **the event, if there is one** → **D+15 after silence** re-entry with something new.
- **Stop after the re-entry.** A fourth attempt converts nobody and costs the user their standing. Silence is an answer; take it gracefully and the door stays open for next year.
- Anchor the sequence to the event if one exists. Everything before it exists to make the handshake land; everything after it exists to convert the handshake.
